Interview brief

The 30-second pitch

UPI handles 24.51 billion transactions a month, so even a tiny failure rate affects millions of people. I wouldn't try to eliminate every failure, because many come from banks and networks Google Pay doesn't control. I'd build a Payment Health & Recovery Engine that detects and classifies each failure, tells the customer what happened and what to do next, blocks unsafe retries, alerts internal teams to failure spikes, and feeds that data back to engineering. The goal: customers always know what's happening to their money.

Executive summary

Digital payments are an everyday necessity in India. UPI processed 24.51 billion transactions in August 2026, worth about ₹29.82 lakh crore, according to NPCI. At this scale, even a small percentage of failed or ambiguous transactions affects millions of payment attempts.

The problem

How can Google Pay reduce the negative impact of payment failures and build greater customer confidence when a transaction cannot be completed successfully?

Some failures originate from banks, payment networks, connectivity or other dependencies Google Pay cannot fully control. So the approach is not to blindly eliminate every technical failure. Instead, build a Payment Failure Detection & Recovery System that:

  • Detects and classifies payment failures.
  • Clearly communicates the problem to the customer.
  • Prevents unnecessary duplicate payments.
  • Automatically alerts internal teams when a failure pattern emerges.
  • Tracks affected customers and transactions.
  • Helps customers safely recover their payment.
  • Generates data engineering teams can use to fix recurring problems.
Core principle

Don't just fix the failure. Fix the customer's experience of the failure.

The problem

A payment is successful when the intended transfer of money is completed. Failures happen in several ways, and the key distinction is this: the payment may fail, but the customer's money may or may not have been debited. NPCI distinguishes business declines, technical declines and debit-reversal or pending scenarios in the UPI ecosystem.

ScenarioWhat happensHow the customer feels
A. Simple failureUser pays, bank rejects, money not debited.Frustrated, but it's straightforward.
B. Technical failureUser pays, bank or server doesn't respond, Google Pay can't confirm.Uncertain: "what happened?"
C. Debit, no confirmationAccount debited, recipient doesn't get confirmation immediately.Anxious about their money.

Why it matters

Customer impact. Payment is a high-intent action: paying a merchant, a friend, rent, a bill or a purchase. A failure interrupts the goal immediately.

Customer trust. Users tolerate an occasional technical problem. What damages trust is uncertainty. They start asking:

  • Did my payment go through?
  • Was my account debited?
  • Should I try again?
  • Will I get my money back?
  • Should I use another payment app?

Payment reliability is therefore not only a technical problem. It is a trust and customer-experience problem.

Business impact. Repeated failures can lead to reduced transaction frequency, higher support volume and cost, more reversals, lower confidence, users switching to other apps or methods, and merchant dissatisfaction.

Root causes

Payment failures fall into four categories.

CategoryExamplesProduct response
Business or user-sideIncorrect UPI PIN, insufficient balance, limit exceeded, invalid beneficiary, account restrictionsBetter error messages, preventive validation, user guidance, contextual instructions
Technical or systemBank downtime, network failure, timeout, bank integration failure, NPCI or infrastructure issuesDetect, inform, prevent unnecessary retries, monitor, alert internal teams, investigate
Pending or unknownBank processes the request but confirmation is delayed, so status is unknownSay "We couldn't confirm your payment yet", then check status before allowing another payment
Debit, no completionAccount debited but the intended payment not completedSay clearly: debited or not, recipient received or not, reversal expected, what to do next
Why the last two matter most

For unknown status, letting the user retry blindly could cause a duplicate payment. Debit without completion creates the highest customer anxiety.

Customer journey

The ideal payment journey looks like this.

  • Initiate
  • Authenticate
  • Bank processing
  • Network
  • Beneficiary bank
  • Success

Failures can happen at multiple points. The product opportunity is to identify where the failure happened and what the customer should do next. A bare "Payment Failed" leaves the user to guess. The proposed message answers three things: what happened, what it means, and what to do next. Use the demo at the top of the page to compare the two.

Decision

The Payment Health & Recovery Engine is the solution to take forward. It unifies the other five into one foundation for the whole payment-failure experience.

The engine classifies every transaction as a business decline, technical failure, pending, unknown, or successful, then shows the right message:

Technical failureYour bank is currently experiencing a technical issue.We couldn't confirm your payment. Please don't make another payment yet. We're checking its status.
Confirmed failurePayment couldn't be completed.Your bank declined the transaction. You can safely try again.
Unknown statusWe're checking your payment.We haven't received confirmation yet. Please don't retry until we confirm the status.
SuccessPayment successfulThe transfer was completed.

The system keeps checking affected transactions until each one resolves.

The feedback loop

This is the most important part of the solution. The product becomes a self-improving reliability system.

  • Failure detected
  • Customer informed
  • Team alerted
  • Root cause found
  • Engineering fix
  • Failure rate falls
  • Better experience
  • More data, better detection

PRD and MVP

Product goal

Reduce the customer impact of UPI payment failures by detecting failures early, communicating clearly, preventing duplicate payments, enabling safe recovery, alerting internal teams and providing actionable failure data.

Target users

Primary: UPI customers. Secondary: customer support, payment operations, backend engineers, bank and PSP integration teams, and risk and fraud teams.

MVP: five components

ComponentWhat it does
Failure classificationIdentify technical, business and pending or unknown failures.
Customer notificationContext-specific messages for each state.
Transaction status checkDetermine whether an uncertain payment eventually succeeded.
Internal alertsDetect abnormal failure spikes.
Basic incident dashboardAffected users and transactions, bank, failure rate, failure type, transaction value, incident duration.
Scope note

Deliberately not in the MVP: alternative payment routing, real-time bank health warnings shown to customers, and advanced preventive validation. They build on the same detection data and can follow once the core loop is proven.

Metrics and guardrails

North Star metric

First-attempt payment success rate = successful first-attempt transactions ÷ total payment attempts. It measures whether customers complete the intended payment without unnecessary retries.

Supporting metricDefinition
Overall transaction success rateSuccessful transactions ÷ total attempts
Technical failure rateTechnical failures ÷ total attempts
Business decline rateBusiness declines ÷ total attempts
Payment recovery rateShare of failed or ambiguous transactions resolved through recovery mechanisms
Customer resolution timeTime from payment failure to clear resolution
Mean time to detectHow quickly the system identifies a payment incident
Mean time to resolveHow quickly the underlying incident is resolved

Guardrail metrics

Improving payment success must not come at the expense of security. Monitor fraud rate and false-positive fraud blocks, duplicate payments, payment latency, support ticket volume, refund and reversal volume, and cost per successful transaction.

We should never increase payment success by weakening fraud or authentication controls.

Roadmap: 12 weeks

  1. DiagnoseWeeks 0–2Analyse historical failures, define the failure taxonomy, establish baselines, identify highest-impact banks and error codes, build the failure funnel.
  2. DetectionWeeks 3–5Failure classification, anomaly detection, bank-level monitoring, failure-threshold alerts.
  3. Customer experienceWeeks 5–8Contextual error messages, pending state, transaction-status checking, customer notifications.
  4. Internal operationsWeeks 8–10Incident dashboard, affected-user tracking, bank-level failure monitoring, transaction-value monitoring.
  5. Experiment and iterateWeeks 10–12A/B or controlled experiments: the existing failure experience against detection, notification and recovery.

Outcome, trade-offs and vision

Expected outcome

I would not invent a specific percentage improvement before seeing baseline data. First establish the baseline across failure volume, failure type, bank-level distribution, recovery rate, support volume, transaction value and resolution time. Then the target is:

Increase first-attempt payment success and payment recovery while reducing customer resolution time and support contacts, without increasing fraud or duplicate payments.

Key trade-offs

Trade-offStance
Fix vs recoverWe can't immediately fix every bank-side failure, so recover the customer experience while engineering works on the underlying issue.
Retry vs duplicate paymentNever retry an uncertain transaction without checking its status.
Success vs securityDon't loosen authentication or fraud controls to raise success. Reliability operates within security guardrails.
Notification vs fatigueNotify only when the information is actionable.

Vision

Build a payment system where customers always know what is happening to their money, and internal teams always know when customers are being affected.

The chain runs from customer to fix: the customer asks "what happened to my payment?", the product answers with what to do, the system shows how many customers are affected, engineering sees which bank is failing, the fix ships, and the customer hears their payment is resolved.

The PM takeaway. A PM doesn't always need to solve the underlying technical problem immediately. The PM needs to understand what is controllable, where the highest leverage exists, and how to protect the customer while the deeper problem is being solved.

Interview Q&A

Likely follow-up questions, with short answers drawn from the reasoning above.

Why not just reduce the failure rate directly?

Many failures come from banks, NPCI and networks outside Google Pay's control. I'd invest in what we control (detection, communication, safe retry, recovery, alerting) and feed failure data to bank and PSP integration teams so root causes get fixed in parallel.

What is the single most important design decision?

Never retry an unknown transaction without checking its status. It's the line between helping the user and creating duplicate payments.

How do you know it worked?

North Star is first-attempt payment success. The experiment's primary metric is payment recovery rate, with secondary metrics for repeat attempts, resolution time and support contacts. Guardrails: duplicate payments, fraud rate and latency.

What would you cut for an MVP?

Five pieces only: classification, contextual notification, status check, spike alerts, and a basic incident dashboard. Alternative routing and early bank health warnings come later.

What if recovery rate rises but duplicate payments also rise?

That's a guardrail breach. I'd pause the rollout, tighten the retry gate so only confirmed failures can be retried, and review the status-check logic before expanding.

What's the biggest risk?

Misclassifying a failure, for example telling a user it's safe to retry when the original payment actually succeeded. Mitigation: enable retry only after a confirmed-failure status, and lean toward the cautious "checking your payment" message.

What target would you set?

I wouldn't state a number before baselining failure volume, bank-level distribution, recovery rate and support volume. I'd set the target after the first two weeks of diagnosis.

How do you avoid notification fatigue?

Notify only when the message is actionable, such as a state change or a retry decision, not for every internal status check.

Who are your stakeholders?

Customers, support, payment operations, backend engineers, bank and PSP integration teams, and risk and fraud teams.
